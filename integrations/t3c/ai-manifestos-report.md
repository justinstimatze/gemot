## Proposed Actions

### 1. Conditional Optimism Protocol

AI manifestos must pair every positive vision claim with a specific, falsifiable condition under which that outcome fails to materialize, and name the institutional mechanism responsible for monitoring it. For example, claims about AI accelerating medical breakthroughs must be coupled with explicit acknowledgment of failure modes (e.g., access inequality, misuse) and a named oversight body. This prevents optimistic framing from functioning as uncritical advocacy while preserving the motivational value of positive vision.

*Requires*: Vision proponents concede that unqualified optimism actively erodes credibility with technical safety audiences and must accept the overhead of conditional framing. Safety advocates concede that positive vision is not inherently dishonest or manipulative and that entirely negative framing fails to build the broad coalitions needed for governance reform.

**Support**: Speaker J Speaker K Speaker L R3, Speaker I R3, Speaker F R3, Speaker H R3
**Opposition**: Speaker A R3, Speaker G R3, Speaker E R3, Speaker C R3

### 2. Separated Document Architecture

Organizations publishing AI manifestos must publish two distinct documents: a public-facing vision document and a separate technical risk register with equal institutional prominence, cross-linked and version-controlled together. Neither document may cite the other selectively without including the full cross-reference. This structural separation prevents safety content from being buried in footnotes while preventing risk framing from dominating public communication.

*Requires*: Optimists concede that burying risks inside vision documents is a form of motivated framing, even if unintentional, and accept the reputational cost of foregrounding a risk register. Safety advocates concede that a standalone risk document paired with a vision document is acceptable, rather than insisting risks dominate the primary narrative.

**Support**: Speaker J Speaker K Speaker L R3, Speaker I R3, Speaker F R3, Speaker H R3
**Opposition**: Speaker C R3, Speaker A R3, Speaker G R3, Speaker E R3

### 3. Credibility-Indexed Vision Claims

Any positive vision claim in an AI manifesto must be tagged with its evidentiary basis on a standardized scale: speculative, emerging evidence, or established finding. Claims tagged speculative must be visually distinguished in the document and cannot appear in executive summaries without that designation. This forces epistemic honesty at the point of publication rather than relying on readers to discount appropriately.

*Requires*: Vision advocates concede that most long-horizon positive claims are genuinely speculative and must accept that labeling them as such will reduce their rhetorical impact in public discourse. Safety advocates concede that speculative positive claims are not inherently misleading if disclosed as such, and that this framework is sufficient rather than demanding removal of forward-looking claims entirely.

**Support**: Speaker C R3, Speaker A R3, Speaker G R3
**Opposition**: Speaker J Speaker K Speaker L R3, Speaker F R3, Speaker H R3

### 4. Third-Party Tension Audit Before Publication

Before a major AI manifesto is released, the publishing organization must commission a structured adversarial review from a Speaker I that includes at least one prominent AI safety researcher and one critic from outside the AI industry, with their critique published simultaneously with the manifesto. The critique need not endorse the manifesto; it must only confirm that the organization engaged with objections in good faith. This creates accountability without giving vetoes to any single constituency.

*Requires*: Optimist organizations concede the reputational risk of simultaneous critical publication and accept that the process may force substantive revisions, not merely cosmetic ones. Safety-oriented reviewers concede that participation in this process does not constitute endorsement and that their role is bounded critique rather than gatekeeping, meaning a manifesto can publish over their objections as long as the critique is visible.

### Synthesis

*LLM-generated synthesis from the final round -- treat as a starting point.*

> Advanced AI development poses alignment risks that are serious enough to require structural safeguards — not merely aspirational commitments. Current oversight methods, including RLHF, have known scaling limits that must be addressed before capabilities cross critical thresholds, and safety frameworks that do not explicitly account for deceptive alignment as a distinct failure mode are incomplete. AI infrastructure access policies must be designed to prevent capture by concentrated private or state actors. These conditions are not optional qualifications on an otherwise positive trajectory — they are prerequisites for any trajectory worth endorsing.
