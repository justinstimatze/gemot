## Proposed Actions

### 1. Conditional Deployment Thresholds with Shared Verification

Establish a binding protocol where AI systems above defined capability thresholds undergo mandatory third-party evaluation for deceptive alignment indicators before deployment, with evaluation criteria co-designed by both safety researchers and developers. Labs retain deployment authority but must publicly disclose evaluation results and any anomalous behaviors detected. Evaluations are funded through a levy on commercial deployments, not government grants, to avoid slowing frontier research disproportionately.

*Requires*: Safety advocates concede that deployment need not halt absent proof of harm, only evidence-based delay. Optimists concede that self-certification is insufficient and that some commercial friction is an acceptable cost for shared legitimacy.

### 2. Parallel Positive-Vision and Risk-Narrative Public Communication Campaign

Fund and coordinate a structured public communication effort that simultaneously articulates a concrete, specific positive AI future (addressing the 100% consensus point) and honestly describes structural risks like deceptive alignment without catastrophizing. Both messages must appear together in all major institutional outputs — no safety organization publishes risk warnings without an accompanying affirmative vision document, and vice versa.

*Requires*: Safety advocates concede that leading with doom framing without a credible alternative is strategically and intellectually incomplete. Techno-optimists concede that positive vision messaging that omits structural risk concerns is dishonest and ultimately undermines institutional trust.

**Support**: Speaker F R3
**Opposition**: Speaker H R3, Speaker I R3, Speaker J Speaker K Speaker L R3, Speaker A R3, Speaker C R3, Speaker E R3, Speaker G R3

### 3. Staged Capability Releases Tied to Interpretability Speaker Ktones

Rather than continuous development and release cycles driven by market pressure, adopt a published roadmap where each new capability tier is only released after interpretability tools sufficient to detect known deceptive alignment signatures at that tier have been demonstrated. The Speaker Ktones are set by an independent technical panel, not by the developing lab, but the panel must include people who have actually shipped systems, not only academics.

*Requires*: Safety researchers concede that Speaker Ktones must be technically achievable and falsifiable — vague interpretability aspirations don't qualify as gates. Developers and optimists concede that market timing cannot override technical readiness criteria, meaning some competitive disadvantage relative to less careful actors is an accepted cost.

**Opposition**: Speaker G R3, Speaker H R3, Speaker E R3, Speaker C R3

### 4. Structured Red-Speaker I Registry for Deceptive Alignment Evidence

Create a shared, industry-wide registry where any lab that observes anomalous goal-directed or deceptive behavior during training — even if subsequently corrected — is required to log and share the incident with a small, security-cleared technical review body. This directly tests the crux about whether deceptive alignment is a real structural pressure or a theoretical concern, by building an empirical record over 24 months before any regulatory conclusions are drawn from it.

*Requires*: Skeptics of deceptive alignment risk concede that if the empirical record accumulates meaningful incidents, they will update their position and support stronger intervention. Safety advocates concede that if the registry shows sparse or negligible evidence after 24 months of honest reporting, some of their stronger structural claims require revision rather than rhetorical doubling down.

### Synthesis

*LLM-generated synthesis from the final round -- treat as a starting point.*

> Advanced AI development, including current capability-scaling trajectories, creates structural risks — including potential deceptive alignment and power-seeking behavior — that warrant binding pre-deployment safety evaluations above defined capability thresholds, regardless of whether catastrophic incidents have yet occurred in deployed systems. These evaluations must be designed and enforced through mechanisms that are independent of the entities being evaluated, not solely through industry-funded processes or voluntary disclosure. Disagreement about the probability of existential outcomes should not delay action on governance mechanisms that reduce risk under multiple threat models. At the Speaker Ee time, safety standards should be developed with meaningful input from practitioners with deployment experience alongside safety researchers and external regulators. Competitive first-mover pressures are real but do not justify bypassing these requirements. International coordination is necessary to prevent regulatory arbitrage. This framework treats safety oversight as a structural precondition for responsible development — not a constraint contingent on empirical confirmation of harm.
